Global temperature

The temperature curve combines Hansen's deep-time global estimate, Osman's 200-year global reconstruction, PAGES2k's annual reconstruction, and GISS monthly observations. Values are relative to 1961–1990. Dashed markers and breaks show where sources change; a jump there can reflect disagreement between reconstructions.

GISS is rebased using its 1961–1990 mean. Hansen uses the author's 14 °C reference. Osman's baseline is estimated from its 150–1750 CE overlap with PAGES2k. These are constant offsets; no variability is stretched or compressed to make sources agree. EPICA's Antarctic temperatures are excluded from the global curve.

Hover to see the temperature source, sample date and available uncertainty. PAGES2k shows a 95% ensemble range; Osman shows one standard deviation. These describe different uncertainty measures and exclude additional uncertainty in joining the sources. Hansen's uncertainty is not quantified in this view. Ancient records cannot resolve the monthly changes visible in modern observations.

The sources use different temperature definitions and methods. For example, GISS blends land air temperatures and sea surface temperatures; Osman reconstructs near-surface air temperature. This is a view of broad climate history, not a homogeneous record for precise rates or lags across joins.

Sea level

The sea-level curve spans geological estimates, a global tide-gauge reconstruction and satellite measurements. Modern values are relative to the 1950 mean; satellite data are aligned using matching months in their overlap with tide gauges. The geological zero-age point provides only an approximate reference. The shaded gap from about 640 to 1807 CE has no connecting line. Ancient estimates are much coarser and less certain. Local coastal samples are excluded.

Atmospheric CO₂

Daily station observations continue the published Antarctic ice-core composite. Lines connect available samples within each source, including days with no observation in between; no missing measurements are added. The source joins remain marked and disconnected.

For deep time, the CenCO₂PIP reconstruction combines multiple proxy methods into one broad trend. We use the authors' January 2024 update: the line shows the median estimate of CO₂ averaged over 500,000 years; the shaded area shows its 95% credible interval. This is uncertainty in the estimated average, not the spread of every measurement or the range of short-term changes. Hover for the bounds and bin midpoint. Zooming in cannot reveal changes finer than these averages. Compare broad trends, not precise peaks or lags against more finely sampled temperature. The original proxy files remain available for reference in the source notes. Dataset license.

Solar irradiance

The older PMIP4 file already combines proxy and sunspot reconstructions with the CMIP6 solar model composite. NNL continues it from 1874; TSIS satellite observations begin in 2018. These source changes are marked. All values are at a fixed Sun–Earth distance of 1 AU, so orbital distance changes are excluded.

Historical models receive constant reference shifts; TSIS keeps its measured level. The shifts compare the same dates, using a median of monthly differences to reduce the influence of exceptional months. The older join uses roughly two solar cycles near the transition. Model trends and cycle amplitudes can still disagree; a shift does not make these one homogeneous record.

When more than 200 years are visible, solar data appear as annual means to match the older annual record. Zooming in restores the daily dates with 50-day means from 1850 onward. Tooltips identify the averaging period, partial years and their coverage. No average blends source joins or fills missing days. Unusual valid readings are retained, and provisional readings stay labelled. Typical solar-cycle changes are around 0.1% of the roughly 1361 W/m² total: automatic vertical scaling makes small changes appear large.

Volcanoes

The published HolVol / eVolv2k / CMIP6 combination is retained, with its component changes marked. Values are annual global aerosol optical depth, rather than eruption counts.

Orbital eccentricity

Published samples are 1,600 years apart, with the latest at 2000 CE, the calculation's reference date. No daily record is available here: zoom out if the panel is empty. Data older than 58 million years are omitted because the provider warns that orbital phase is unconstrained there.

World population

Historical estimates run through 2023. Values from 2024 are projections and appear as dashed lines, even for years that have already passed. Only years through the current calendar year are shown. Lines connect annual values within each segment; they do not imply daily measurements or extend beyond the newest sample. Population is not included in the automatic observation updates.

Interpreting correlations

Visual agreement helps you ask questions, but does not establish causation. Some reconstructions use climate models and shared inputs: Osman uses model priors that include greenhouse-gas forcing. Its relationship with CO₂ is not a fully independent test. Miller's sea-level estimates also use temperature-related inputs, which can influence their agreement with temperature.
